title: Case scenario on Lymphoma
---

A 60 year old male came with the complaints of weakness & fatigability for 1 month with fever and drenching night sweat for same duration. On examination patient is anemic with cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy.


For a 60-year-old male presenting with weakness, B symptoms (fever, drenching night sweats), and lymphadenopathy, we must act swiftly. This triad strongly points towards hematologic malignancy or chronic, systemic infection.

Here is a concise breakdown of the vital differential diagnoses and the essential initial steps, হরিবোল-style:

### Differential Diagnosis (DDx)

The combination of **fever, night sweats, fatigue/weakness, and lymphadenopathy** is highly suspicious, often referred to as "B symptoms" when weight loss is present (and fatigue/weakness are usually concomitant).

#### 1. Hematologic Malignancy (Top Priority)
Malignant neoplasms, especially lymphomas and leukemias, are major causes in patients presenting with fever of unknown origin (FUO).

*   **Lymphoma (Hodgkin’s and Non-Hodgkin’s Lymphoma):** This is the most common malignancy causing FUO.
    *   **Hodgkin’s Lymphoma (HL):** Approximately one-third of patients present with B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss). The adenopathy often involves cervical and axillary regions. Rarely, it presents with Pel-Ebstein fever.
    *   **Non-Hodgkin’s Lymphoma (NHL):** The presence of B symptoms is key in assessing progression. Diffuse adenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and constitutional symptoms are common in Angioimmunoblastic T-Cell Lymphoma.
*   **Leukemia (Acute Myeloid Leukemia - AML):** Fatigue is a frequent first symptom, and fever (with or without infection) is the initial symptom in about 10% of patients.
*   **Multiple Myeloma:** Can present with fatigue and anemia.

#### 2. Chronic/Systemic Infections

Chronic or disseminated infections must be thoroughly excluded, as they often mimic malignancy.

*   **Tuberculosis (TB):** Systemic features in pulmonary TB often include fever (low-grade and intermittent) and night sweats. Extrapulmonary TB, such as miliary TB or lymphadenitis (especially cervical), can cause FUO.
*   **Chronic Q Fever:** Often presents with nonspecific symptoms like fever, night sweats, weight loss, and fatigue, sometimes without acute infection being recognized.
*   **Brucellosis:** Characterized by undulating fever, sweats, weight loss, myalgia, and lymphadenopathy (10-20% of cases).
*   **Infectious Mononucleosis (EBV/CMV):** While rarer in older adults, IM in the elderly can present with prolonged fever, fatigue, myalgia, and malaise, often sparing classic symptoms like pharyngitis and marked adenopathy seen in younger patients.

#### 3. Inflammatory/Autoimmune Disorders

Systemic inflammatory disorders frequently present with fever, fatigue, and anemia.

*   **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) or Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA):** Can cause generalized lymphadenopathy and systemic inflammation.
*   **Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA):** Characterized by fever, anemia (normochromic/slightly hypochromic), high ESR/CRP, fatigue, and weight loss in patients >50 years old.

### Key Clinical Findings Explained

| **Weakness/Fatigability** | Universal symptom of systemic illness. Highly correlated with underlying inflammation, malignancy, and anemia. | Fatigue is a prominent manifestation of systemic syndromes and common in cancer. Anemia causes tiredness and shortness of breath. |
| **Anemia** | In a 60-year-old, common causes are iron deficiency and Anemia of Chronic Disease (ACD). ACD is strongly linked to underlying inflammatory disorders, infections, and cancer. | Anemia is not a normal age-related change. Iron deficiency (low ferritin) must be distinguished from ACD (normal/high ferritin). |
| **Lymphadenopathy (Cervical/Axillary)** | Involvement of these nodes suggests spread from regional infection/injury or, more concerningly, a systemic process. | Cervical and axillary nodes are frequent sites of involvement for lymphomas and systemic infections. Enlarged supraclavicular nodes are *always* considered abnormal and drain the chest/retroperitoneum. |

### Initial Diagnostic Approach

For this presentation, the workup needs to address all cardinal manifestations—especially differentiating malignancy/chronic infection from routine FUO.

**1. Obligatory Investigations (Minimum FUO Workup):** Since the duration is 1 month, this qualifies as a prolonged febrile illness and requires thorough investigation.

*   **Hematology:** Complete Blood Count (CBC) with differential, platelet count, hemoglobin, ESR, and C-reactive protein (CRP) level. Anemia workup (Reticulocyte count, Iron/Ferritin/TIBC) is essential to classify the anemia type (e.g., iron deficiency vs. ACD).
*   **Biochemistry:** Electrolytes, creatinine, liver enzymes (ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, LDH), creatine kinase, ferritin.
*   **Immunology/Serology (Obligatory):** Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and rheumatoid factor (RF).
*   **Infection Screening:** Blood cultures (n=3), urine culture, tuberculin skin test (TST) or Interferon $\gamma$ release assay (IGRA).

**2. Guided Imaging/Further Tests:**

*   **Imaging:** Chest X-ray and abdominal ultrasonography are required obligatory investigations. Given the cervical/axillary adenopathy, chest CT scan or CT-PET should be considered early to look for thoracic or abdominal involvement, especially if lymphoma is suspected.
*   **Lymph Node Biopsy:** If the history and initial tests point strongly to malignancy or granulomatous disease (like TB), especially if the nodes are large (e.g., >2 cm in diameter) or hard/fixed, an excisional biopsy is critical for diagnosis.
*   **Advanced Serology:** Consider testing for specific chronic infections based on exposure history (e.g., HIV, Hepatitis B/C, Toxoplasmosis, Brucellosis, Q fever).

**Vital Point to Remember:** Stopping unnecessary antibiotics and glucocorticoids is essential before further diagnostic tests, as they can mask diseases and decrease the reliability of cultures and node size measurements. Rule out fraudulent fever using simultaneous temperature measurements and check for temperature-pulse dissociation.

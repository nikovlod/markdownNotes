---
title: Case scenario of acute viral hepatitis 
---

A 15 year old boy came with the complaints of weakness, nausea and vomiting for 10 days. On examination patient is icteric and there is mild tenderness in right hypochondriac region. Investigation reveals S. Bilirubin 7.2mg/dl, SGPT 1245 IU/L, ALP 177 IU/L.

Based on the history, physical examination, and key laboratory findings, the diagnosis is highly suggestive of **Acute Viral Hepatitis**.

Here is the breakdown of why this is the most probable diagnosis:

### Clinical Rationale

1.  **Acute Onset and Prodromal Symptoms:** The patient presents with classic prodromal symptoms of acute viral hepatitis, which include weakness, loss of appetite (anorexia, implied by weakness/malaise), nausea, and vomiting for 10 days. Jaundice often appears as the illness progresses.
2.  **Physical Findings:**
    *   The patient is icteric (jaundiced).
    *   The presence of mild tenderness in the right hypochondriac region (right upper abdomen) is common in acute viral hepatitis. The liver in acute viral hepatitis is typically enlarged, smooth, soft in consistency, and tender.

### Laboratory Rationale

The pattern of the liver function tests (LFTs) strongly indicates acute hepatocellular injury (hepatitis), rather than obstruction or hemolysis:

| Lab Value | Patient Value | Normal Pattern in Sources | Implication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **SGPT (ALT)** | 1245 IU/L (Very High) | Markedly high aminotransferases are characteristic. | Confirms severe hepatocellular damage. |
| **ALP** | 177 IU/L (Mildly High) | Elevated, but often only slightly high compared to transaminases. | Rules against pure obstructive jaundice, where ALP is usually *very high*. |
| **S. Bilirubin** | 7.2 mg/dL (Moderately High) | Jaundice is present. This level is higher than usually seen in hemolytic jaundice (typically < 6 mg/dL or 100 µmol/L). | Consistent with hepatic jaundice, which involves both unconjugated and conjugated bilirubin. |

### Vital Points to Remember

1.  **Exclusion of Obstructive Jaundice:** Obstructive jaundice typically presents with severe generalized itching (pruritus) and pale or clay-colored stools, and is usually *not* preceded by anorexia, nausea, and vomiting. Furthermore, Obstructive Jaundice typically causes a disproportionately high Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP). Your patient’s presentation lacks these key obstructive features.
2.  **Exclusion of Hemolytic Jaundice:** Hemolytic jaundice is usually mild, serum bilirubin is typically less than 6 mg/dL, and key liver enzymes (SGPT/ALT, ALP) are usually normal. Constitutional symptoms like anorexia and vomiting are usually absent. Your patient has high SGPT and significant nausea/vomiting, ruling this out.
3.  **Hepatitis Investigation:** For confirmation, you would need viral markers (HBsAg, anti-HBc IgM for Hepatitis B, anti-HAV IgM for Hepatitis A, anti-HEV, etc.) to identify the specific virus. In acute infection, high titers of anti-HBc IgM are diagnostic for Hepatitis B, even if HBsAg is cleared rapidly.
